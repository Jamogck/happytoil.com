## The business

A livestock farm with several enterprises running at once — pastured poultry, a small
beef herd, seasonal produce — and one household trying to decide whether the whole
thing could replace an off-farm job.

## What I found

Production was the part that worked. The farming was competent and the customers were
loyal. Everything around the farming was improvised: orders arrived by text message,
Facebook comment, and roadside conversation, then got copied into a notebook that
travelled in a truck. Pricing had been set once and adjusted only when a customer
complained.

The more interesting problem was that nobody could say which enterprise made money. Feed
was bought in bulk and split by feel. Labor wasn’t counted at all, on the theory that
it was the family’s own time — which is exactly the assumption that keeps a farm from
becoming a job.

## What mattered

Not the order system, though that’s what I was asked about. The real question was which
two enterprises deserved the household’s full attention, and that question couldn’t be
answered until the costs were separated.

## What changed

We built the cost picture first — one season of honest allocation, including labor at a
wage the owner would actually accept from someone else. Two enterprises turned out to be
carrying the third. One was near-break-even at twice the effort.

Then the ordinary work: a simple order system that the customers could use and the owner
could run from a phone, a pricing model tied to input costs rather than to what the
neighbors charge, and a seasonal plan that started from the household’s income
requirement and worked backwards to bird counts.

## What resulted

The farm dropped an enterprise, raised prices on another, and stopped taking orders by
text. The off-farm job ended the following year.

## What I learned

The tooling question is almost never the question. If I’d built the order system I was
hired to build and gone home, the farm would have been more organized and just as stuck.
