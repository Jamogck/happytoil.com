## The business

A food producer selling through grocery and direct channels, running one plant near
capacity, with a landlord offering a second building on favorable terms.

## What I found

The expansion case rested on a volume assumption borrowed from the existing plant’s best
year. It also assumed the second location would run at the same labor efficiency, which
quietly required the person who runs the first plant to be in two places.

Nobody was being dishonest. The model just hadn’t been asked the uncomfortable questions,
because everyone involved wanted the answer to be yes.

## What mattered

Not whether to expand. Whether the business could staff a second location without
degrading the first — and how long the first plant could absorb demand if they waited.

## What changed

We built a version of the model with the key person’s time as an explicit constraint,
and ran the case where volume arrives twelve months later than hoped. Under those
assumptions the expansion was survivable but tight, and the tightness landed exactly on
the operation that was currently working well.

We also priced the alternative nobody had modeled: a second shift at the existing plant,
which was less exciting and considerably cheaper.

## What resulted

The owners waited a year, added the shift, and hired and trained the person who would
eventually run the second plant. They opened it later, from a stronger position.

## What I learned

Part of the job is being the person in the room who is not excited. That’s easier to do
when you’re not trying to sell the next phase of work — which is one reason I keep the
practice small.
