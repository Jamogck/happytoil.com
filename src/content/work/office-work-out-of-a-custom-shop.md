## The business

A fabrication shop doing one-off and short-run work for contractors and manufacturers.
Eleven people, a good reputation, and more inquiries than it could answer.

## What I found

The owner was the only person who could produce a quote, and he was producing them at
night. Each one started from a blank page. Two jobs of nearly identical scope, quoted
four months apart, differed by a third — not because anything had changed, but because
estimating from memory drifts.

The shop was also quoting everything. Every inquiry got the same effort, including the
ones from buyers who were collecting three bids to satisfy a procurement rule.

## What mattered

Two things, in this order: deciding what not to quote, and making a quote something more
than one person could produce.

## What changed

We spent a week reconstructing the last two years of quotes against what the jobs
actually cost to build. That produced something more useful than a rate card — a small
set of job archetypes with real hours attached, and a clear picture of which customers
were reliably profitable.

From there: a qualification step at the front, so inquiries that didn’t fit got a polite
no within a day; a set of quote templates built on the archetypes; and eventually a
modest internal tool so the shop lead could produce most estimates without the owner.

## What resulted

Quote volume fell. Win rate rose. The owner stopped estimating at night, which was the
outcome his wife cared about and, as it turned out, the one that mattered most.

## What I learned

Saying no to work is a system, not a personality trait. Shops that can’t do it aren’t
undisciplined — they usually just have no agreed way to tell a good inquiry from a bad
one, so every no feels like a judgment call made under pressure.
