Ask an owner-operator what a thing costs to make and you’ll usually get a real answer:
materials, packaging, a share of the utilities, maybe freight. Ask whether their own hours
are in there and you’ll get a version of the same shrug. *That’s just my time.*

It’s an honest habit. When you’re starting, your labor genuinely is the free input — the
thing you have instead of capital. Leaving it out is how the business gets off the ground.

The trouble is that the habit outlives the situation. Years later the business is real,
the hours are still uncounted, and every decision that depends on knowing what things
cost is being made against a number with a hole in it.

## What it hides

Three things, mostly.

It hides which products are worth making. Two items with the same margin on paper can
differ by a factor of four in hours. Uncounted, they look identical, and the business
keeps making the one that’s quietly eating the week.

It hides whether you can hire. The moment you pay someone to do what you’ve been doing
free, the cost structure changes — unless it was already in the model, in which case
hiring is just a swap. Businesses that never counted their own labor tend to experience
their first employee as an inexplicable collapse in profitability.

And it hides the wage. If you’re working sixty hours and the business clears a modest
profit, you may be paying yourself less than the job pays elsewhere. That can be a fine
choice. It should be a choice.

## What to do about it

Pick the wage you’d have to pay someone competent to do the work — not the wage you wish
you earned, and not minimum wage because you feel guilty. Put it in the model at the
enterprise level for one season. That’s enough.

You don’t have to like the result. Most people find one product they’ll stop making, one
price they’ll raise, and one task they’ll hand off sooner than planned. That’s the whole
return on the exercise, and it’s usually worth more than anything else you’d do with the
same afternoon.
