Every business I’ve worked with has one. It’s called something like `MASTER copy FINAL`,
it lives on one person’s desktop, and it has a tab nobody dares delete. When I’m brought
in to build software, the spreadsheet is usually presented as the thing to be replaced —
proof that the business has outgrown its improvisations.

That’s the wrong way to meet it. The spreadsheet is not a failure of process. It is the
process, written down by the person closest to the work, revised every time reality
disagreed with it. Nothing else in the business has been tested that hard.

## What it knows

Read one carefully and you’ll find the business’s real rules, the ones that never made it
into a policy. A column that looks redundant is usually load-bearing. A hard-coded number
in the corner is somebody’s hard-won correction to a formula that was wrong for two
seasons. The three rows highlighted yellow are the customers who pay late, recorded
without anyone deciding to record it.

You’ll also find the shape of the actual workflow, which is rarely the workflow anyone
describes out loud. Order of operations, who touches what, where the exceptions go. This
is the requirements document you were going to spend six weeks producing.

## What it costs

The case against it is real. One person holds it, which makes them a single point of
failure and, more quietly, traps them: they can’t take a week off without the business
slowing down. It doesn’t scale past the point where two people need it open at once. And
its errors are silent — a formula that stops covering a row is not an outage, it’s a
number that’s slightly wrong for a year.

The trouble is that these costs arrive gradually while the benefits were immediate, so
businesses tend to stay past the point where the trade stopped being good.

## Replacing it

When the time comes, the mistake is to build the system you were asked for. Build the
spreadsheet’s understanding of the business instead — including the parts that look like
mess. Then remove what turns out to be scar tissue, deliberately, one thing at a time,
with the person who built it in the room.

And leave a way out. Whatever replaces it should export cleanly, because in four years
somebody will need to do something the software doesn’t do, and they’ll do it in a
spreadsheet.
