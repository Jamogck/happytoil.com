Body text is Source Serif 4 at a comfortable size on a measure of roughly sixty-five
characters. The line length matters more than the size: prose set across a wide screen is
tiring to read no matter how handsome the face, which is why the reading column here stops
where it does and the rest of the grid stays empty.

Ordinary emphasis is *italic*, and **bold** exists for the rare occasion when a phrase has
to be findable by someone skimming. Inline links look [like this](/about/) — near-black
text with a green underline — so they’re visible in a paragraph without shouting.[^measure]

## A subhead, set in the serif

Subheads use the same face as the body at a modest size increase. They mark a turn in the
argument rather than announcing a new product feature. Two levels are available, which is
one more than most essays need.

### A third level, set in the sans

Below the second level the type switches to IBM Plex Sans, which reads as an annotation on
the argument rather than a division of it.

<MarginNote>
  Marginal notes sit beside the text on wide screens and fold into the column on narrow
  ones. Good for an aside that would break the sentence if it were parenthetical.
</MarginNote>

Lists are set with a small square marker in the deep green — one of the few places brand
color appears outside the mark:

- A first item, kept to a sentence or so.
- A second item, which may run longer without looking wrong, because the marker sits
  outside the measure and the text stays aligned.
- A third.

> A block quote is indented behind a hairline and set in italic. It’s for someone else’s
> words, at length — a customer, a book, a report that said something better than a
> paraphrase would.

<PullQuote cite="A line worth repeating, attributed if it isn’t mine">
  A pull quote is a line from the essay itself, given room and rules. Use it once, maybe
  twice — never as decoration for a paragraph that didn’t earn it.
</PullQuote>

<Callout label="A note">
  Callouts hold a caveat, a definition, or context a reader may not need. They sit beside
  the argument, marked with a yellow rule, and stay short.
</Callout>

## Technical matter

Tables are set in the sans, ruled only where a rule helps, and read as though they came out
of a notebook rather than a marketing deck.

| Enterprise | Hours per week | Gross margin | Notes                          |
| ---------- | -------------- | ------------ | ------------------------------ |
| Broilers   | 22             | 41%          | Seasonal, May–October          |
| Beef       | 6              | 33%          | Sold by the half               |
| Produce    | 31             | 12%          | Labor not fully counted        |

Code, when an essay needs it, is set in a monospace face at a slightly smaller size:

```js
const portion = work + food + drink + company;
```

Footnotes collect at the bottom, numbered, in the smaller sans.[^ecclesiastes] They’re the
right home for a source, a qualification, or an aside that would otherwise interrupt.

[^measure]: Sixty to seventy characters is the range most typographers land on. This site
    sits near the upper end because the serif is generous and the leading is loose.

[^ecclesiastes]: The name is an allusion, not a thesis. There’s a fuller account of it on
    the [about page](/about/).
